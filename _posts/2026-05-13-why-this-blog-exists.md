I have learned a few things about adulthood.

First, two suitcases are apparently enough to relocate an entire life. Or at least that is what airline baggage policies would like you to believe.

Second, production systems have impeccable timing. They rarely fail when you are comfortably seated near Wi-Fi and emotionally available. They do, however, develop a deep sense of urgency the moment you are sweaty, hungry, nowhere near a charger, and trying to find food in a city you barely know. Chaos, as it turns out, is highly punctual.

Third, every carefully designed career plan eventually becomes improvisation.

Before Missouri, before consulting, before diagrams, dashboards, and long conversations involving phrases like "business alignment," there were books.

Too many books, according to airline baggage rules.

Not the curated kind that sit neatly on shelves to signal personality. The kind that quietly become part of who you are. The kind that travels with you long before you ever leave home.

Nancy Drew. Famous Five. Hardy Boys. Alistair MacLean. Robert Ludlum. Stephen King. Paulo Coelho. Thrillers. Philosophy. Stories. The sort that quietly convince you that curiosity is a reasonable way to move through the world.

Outside class, life looked far less stereotypically technical. I loved Bharatanatyam, volleyball, philosophy, and the strange satisfaction of disappearing into ideas for long stretches of time. Somewhere between movement, books, and curiosity, I developed a habit of asking why before how. That habit, quietly and persistently, would follow me into every version of adulthood.

Long before phrases like AI, automation, or context-aware systems became fashionable, I spent my college years building a thesis around an oddly specific question: What if a home could recognize who entered a room and quietly adapt around them? Lights. Temperature. Devices. Preferences. A home with memory.

Looking back, it feels suspiciously close to the kinds of systems we now describe using much more impressive terminology. At the time, though, it was simply curiosity and an early attempt at understanding invisible systems and the people moving through them.

I grew up between my hometown in North Karnataka and later Bangalore, stubborn in ways that were occasionally admirable and occasionally exhausting for everyone around me. Once I decided something, changing my mind was nearly impossible, a trait that would later help me convince my parents to let me move halfway across the world and, in retrospect, explain several other questionable but formative decisions.

I liked understanding how things worked, but life had a habit of forcing questions I had not planned for. People changed plans. Situations changed priorities. Certainties quietly disappeared. I was rarely ready for those moments, though I have since learned that most meaningful turning points do not arrive looking particularly meaningful. At the time, they mostly feel inconvenient, confusing, and mildly unfair.

By the time college ended and corporate life began, I found myself in Bangalore working in software and enterprise systems which were equal parts technical problem-solving, structured processes, and learning how organizations quietly function behind PowerPoint presentations. I liked building things. Solving problems. Watching messy systems somehow hold together despite themselves.

But somewhere between software releases, stakeholder meetings, and the slow realization that adulthood mostly involves carrying a laptop "just in case," a question started following me around: Did I want to stay only technical?

I liked technology, but I also liked conversations. Decisions. Ambiguity. Why perfectly reasonable humans gathered in conference rooms and somehow left with dramatically unreasonable processes. I found myself increasingly drawn to the messy middle where business problems became technical ones and technical decisions quietly shaped business outcomes.

I wanted to be closer to that. Which is how I eventually convinced my parents and perhaps more importantly, myself to move halfway across the world for an MBA. You can fit surprisingly little of your emotional history into airline-approved luggage.

Two 23-kilo bags, one smaller suitcase, and a personal item were somehow expected to contain a life stretched between my hometown, Bangalore, routines, prayers, ambitions, uncertainty, and an unreasonable number of books.

My mother cried at the airport. I tried not to, for the most part. We did not even pretend to be good at emotional restraint. Somewhere between security checks, last-minute instructions, and the universal parent expression of "eat properly and call me," adulthood became unexpectedly real.

And then came silence.

When I landed in St. Louis, Missouri, everything felt strangely quiet.

Not cinematic. Not dramatic. Just quiet.

The kind of silence that arrives when you are emotionally overwhelmed, physically exhausted, mildly jet-lagged, and suddenly aware that your support system now exists several time zones away.

It was terrifying. Exciting. Mostly disorienting.

I expected culture shock to arrive in dramatic ways. Weather. Food. Distance. Accents.

Instead, it arrived in small, deeply confusing moments.

People smiled at strangers. They said "good morning" without apparently needing a reason. Cashiers asked, "How are you?" and, to my great confusion, occasionally waited for an answer. Doors were held open. Small conversations materialized unexpectedly.

Growing up in India, kindness existed, of course, but it looked different, more contextual, more familiar, less conversational with strangers standing near cereal aisles.

Missouri introduced me to a quieter, everyday politeness that took some getting used to.

Business school turned out to be less about business and more about people.

I found myself working across projects that forced me to understand markets, communication, persuasion, organizations, and the subtle ways context changes behavior. I worked across international trade and marketing, contributed to consulting projects, and slowly realized something important: I genuinely liked translating complexity for people.

Somewhere in the middle of presentations, spreadsheets, strategy discussions, and trying to sound far more confident than I often felt, I realized something uncomfortable but useful:

I did not want to choose between technical depth and people-facing work.

I wanted both.

Around the same time, I worked with a growing startup where I spent time understanding warehouses, construction equipment, dashboards, and the strange beauty of operational systems.

I walked warehouse floors. Watched how things moved. Observed how decisions happened and then saw all of it eventually become numbers on dashboards consumed by executives.

For the first time, I could clearly see the chain: real-world messiness → systems → data → decisions

Something shifted.

The dashboard mattered because someone was trying to decide. The process mattered because someone somewhere depended on it. Data mattered because imperfect, hurried, ambitious humans were using it to understand reality.

After business school, theory met reality.

Reality, unsurprisingly, had deadlines.

I began working in corporate America as a data engineer: a role that sounded very sophisticated and occasionally was, but mostly involved learning systems, solving problems, and discovering that enterprise technology is held together by a fascinating combination of logic, urgency, optimism, and institutional memory.

For the first time, I saw how organizations moved work, decisions, and information. Warehouses, deliveries, dashboards, pipelines, operational systems and suddenly everything felt connected.

Every company had its own ecosystem: different jargon, tools, personalities, and politics, yet somehow all shared one truth: There is always one spreadsheet everyone secretly depends on.

Later, working in mortgage and lending systems, I learned something that would quietly follow me into every role after that: Technology only makes sense when you understand the business underneath it. Behind every approval sat documents, rules, risks, tradeoffs, and deeply human decisions quietly shaping people's lives.

Eventually, consulting across industries gave me something I had unknowingly been searching for: variety.

Curiosity became useful.

Asking too many questions became a professional skill.

And somewhere along the way, I found myself increasingly comfortable standing in the messy middle between business and technology.

A few years later, I moved to the East Coast looking for change: more industries, more complexity, faster-moving environments, bigger questions. Somewhere between finance, construction, manufacturing, logistics, architecture diagrams, executive conversations, and systems that always seemed slightly messier than they first appeared, my view of work widened.

Then something changed again. Life happened.

The job market I encountered felt very different from the one I had entered only a few years earlier. Somewhere between 2022 and 2026, the ground shifted. The assumptions that once felt stable suddenly felt negotiable. Experience alone no longer felt sufficient. Curiosity, oddly enough, became necessary again.

It felt strangely familiar.

Like the curious kid still trying to understand how things worked. Like the student building a smart home system because a question felt too interesting to ignore. Like the person who once believed professional experience alone would somehow be enough.

Somewhere inside the uncertainty, I found unexpected clarity.

The systems were evolving, and so, inconveniently, was I supposed to.

Again, meaningful change arrived in ways that did not feel good. Or pleasant. But it pushed me toward things I had once assumed belonged in some distant future. Suddenly, tools that felt almost unreal became systems capable of coding, reasoning, brainstorming, explaining, and challenging assumptions. They were the quiet collaborators in learning. Claude Code. ChatGPT. Gemini. The kinds of peers I once would have assumed belonged another hundred years away.

This blog, unexpectedly, became part of that evolution. Built with curiosity, helped along by technology, and shaped by the slightly uncomfortable realization that standing still is rarely a strategy when the systems around you are changing.

Somewhere between books, airports, warehouses, dashboards, conference rooms, job searches, and systems with suspiciously excellent timing for failure, I realized something:

I genuinely enjoy understanding how things work.

Not just technology.

People. Decisions. Organizations. Why technically elegant systems fail because humans do not behave like flowcharts. Why every industry believes its problems are uniquely complicated while quietly struggling with remarkably familiar ones. Why learning, inconveniently, never seems to finish.

This blog is my attempt to make that learning a little easier for myself and, hopefully, for anyone else trying to make sense of technology, systems, AI, careers, ambiguity, or the strange reality that adulthood increasingly feels like improvisation with better tools.

I am still figuring things out.

Still opinionated. Still ambitious. Still asking why before how.

Mostly, still curious.

If any of that sounds familiar, welcome.
